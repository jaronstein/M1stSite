We call the distance between what an ad promises and what its landing page delivers the **message-match gap**. To see how much it actually costs brands, we analyzed the Meta advertising of 22 leading DTC brands: reading what each ad promises (the words baked into the creative, the words spoken in the video) and checking, piece by piece, whether the destination page delivers it.

This isn't a vibe check. Every ad-to-page pair gets broken into the specific promises the ad makes: the **primary promise**, the **offer**, **specific claims**, **audience signals**, **tone**, **urgency**, and **social proof**. A page either carries each one or it doesn't. A promise the page drops is a **gap**.

Then we ranked all 22 brands by how many gaps they rack up per ad. The distance between the top and the bottom isn't subtle.

Quince runs a warm, word-of-mouth style ad: *"My husband loves these linen shirts from Quince."* Click it, and you land on the exact product being talked about, the 100% European Linen Relaxed Short Sleeve Shirt, with the whole promise settled before you scroll:

- The exact shirt from the ad, front and center.
- **$34, marked down from $98** ("you save 65%") right next to the price.
- **3,056 reviews**, a **"Best seller"** flag, free shipping, and 365-day returns, all above the fold.

The ad made a promise. The page paid it off, in full, instantly. Across all five of Quince's ads, we found **8 gaps total, 1.6 per ad**, and every one of them was cosmetic: a missing trust badge here, a slightly under-specified audience cue there. Quince never drops the promise, never drops the offer, never leaves a visitor wondering if they're in the right place.

Bombas ties Quince for the fewest gaps in the entire index (also 1.6 per ad). The pattern at the top is consistent: **the core message always survives the click.**

Fabletics runs a genuinely good, specific ad:

> "Introducing Vintage Grey, your new go-with-everything scrubs color this summer! For a limited time, get your first scrub set for just $15 as a new VIP signup."

A color. A season. A versatility angle. A price. Click it, and you don't land on Vintage Grey. You land on a generic "join VIP" membership page, greeted by a gamified pop-up ("A gift just for you, tap a gift box to unwrap your discount") sitting on top of the hero. Vintage Grey survives as one swatch in a row of ten. The "go-with-everything" pitch, the summer story, the specific reason you clicked: gone. The ad sold a product. The page sells a subscription.

That's the pattern for every Fabletics ad we looked at: a sharp, specific hook poured into the same one-size-fits-all funnel. Across their five ads we counted **33 gaps, 6.6 per ad**, roughly **four times** Quince's rate. And these aren't cosmetic. Six or seven of every ad's eight promises were gaps.

## The Pattern Holds Across the Index

Zoom out from the two extremes and the difference between the best and worst halves is structural, not random:

- **The best brands never drop the load-bearing pieces.** Across the six lowest-gap brands, we found zero dropped offers and only a handful of weak primary-promise or tone components. Their misses are refinements.
- **The worst brands drop exactly those pieces.** Across the six highest-gap brands, the most common gaps were the primary promise, the tone and identity of the brand, and the offer itself: the things a visitor needs to see in the first two seconds to believe they're in the right place.

Message match isn't a tie-breaker that separates good stores from great ones. It's the difference between a page that confirms the click and a page that silently asks the visitor to start over.

### How It Closes a Gap: One Real Example

The client ran a Father's Day ad: your dad wants a new polo, plus the gear from their latest sneaker-brand collab. The collection page it linked to led with a generic "the new drop is now live, shop now" banner. None of the gifting occasion. None of the collab. Five gaps, one for each promise the ad made and the page ignored.

Throughline's fix didn't invent new marketing copy. It borrowed the ad's own words back onto the page, the principle the whole engine runs on:

- **Primary promise:** a hero subhead delivering the Father's Day gifting line the ad opened with, above the fold.
- **Urgency:** the announcement bar rewritten from "now live" to the time-sensitive Father's Day occasion the ad established.
- **Audience and tone:** a short intro reinstating the gift-buyer framing and the brand's playful voice.
- **Specific claim:** a callout naming the exact sneaker collaboration the ad led with.

Four of the five gaps closed. Same products, same brand, same page. Now it finishes the sentence the ad started.
